# 4.3 | Cowork Typical Use Cases

## Learning Objectives

- Understand, through case studies, several typical office scenarios that Cowork is best suited to solve;
- Be able to judge "is the task in front of me a good fit for Cowork";
- Build a scenario map for the in-depth hands-on lessons in 4.4–4.6, making it easy to match the right approach to the right task.

## Suggested Duration

20 minutes (primarily case-study driven)

## Prerequisites

- Have completed 4.1 and 4.2, and are familiar with basic Cowork operations and feature modules.

## Course Outline

Use cases instead of abstract descriptions, explaining each one in turn; each scenario corresponds to a subsequent lesson where it will be explored in depth:

| Scenario | Typical Task | In-Depth Lesson |

### II. Judgment Criteria: What Tasks Are Suited to Cowork

Give learners a simple self-check list:

- [ ] The task involves multiple files/pieces of data, rather than a single Q&A;
- [ ] The task has a clearly defined "completion state" (rather than being an open-ended, unlimited chat);
- [ ] The task may require your judgment at key points during the process (rather than being fully automated batch processing — that scenario is better suited to the automation scripts in Chapter 5);
- [ ] The output needs to be presented in a structured form such as a document/spreadsheet/report.

If 2 or more of these are met, it is generally a good fit for Cowork.

### III. Quick Case Study: Organizing Meeting Minutes (Brief Demo, Not the Main Focus of This Lesson)

Give a live demonstration of a lightweight case so learners get a feel for what "typical scenarios" look like in actual operation:

1. Provide a sample meeting transcript (which can be colloquial and structurally messy in its raw form);
2. Enter the task:

```
> Please organize this meeting transcript into structured minutes, including: discussion topics, key conclusions, and action items (including owner and deadline, if mentioned)
```

3. Observe the output and discuss which pieces of information were explicitly stated in the original text and which were reasonably inferred by the AI — emphasize that inferred content needs to be double-checked and should not be blindly trusted.

### IV. Combining Scenarios Together

Emphasize that scenarios don't exist in isolation — in real work they often appear in combination, for example:

> "Organize this batch of customer interview transcripts (file organization) → extract key data into a comparison table (spreadsheet organization) → combine with industry background for further analysis (market research) → sync the final report to the team's Feishu document (cross-platform collaboration)"

This is exactly the complete chain that 4.4–4.7 will break down step by step. This lesson first establishes a "big-picture map" of awareness, to prevent learners from "seeing only the trees, not the forest" in later lessons.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Should all office tasks use Cowork?**
A: No. For a very simple single question (e.g., "help me translate this sentence"), a regular conversation is faster; Cowork's value is fully realized in scenarios involving multiple files, multiple steps, and the need for process management. Choosing the right tool matters more than always using the "most advanced" one.

**Q: What's the difference between Cowork and the automation scripts in Chapter 5?**
A: Cowork is suited to one-off or low-frequency tasks that "require human judgment to intervene and are non-repetitive"; the automation in Chapter 5 is better suited to tasks that are "clearly rule-based, can run fully unattended, and need to be repeated." The two are complementary rather than substitutes — the specific differences will be compared again at the start of Chapter 5.
