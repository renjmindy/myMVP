## Course Outline

### I. The Context Window: A Fundamental Principle That Must Be Understood

- In every conversation, the amount of information Claude can "see" is limited — this upper limit is called the Context Window;
- As a conversation grows longer and more files are read, earlier information may be compressed or marginalized. This is why long conversations sometimes "forget things" or become "inconsistent" later on;
- This is also why `/clear` and `/compact` learned in Chapter 1, as well as `CLAUDE.md` and Sub Agent splitting learned in Chapter 2, all matter — they are all, in essence, ways of **managing a limited context resource**.

**Analogy:** the context window is like "the size of your desk surface." The messier the desk and the more piled on it, the less efficient you are at finding things and getting work done. `/clear` is "clearing the desk and starting fresh," `/compact` is "consolidating what's on the desk into a compact summary," `CLAUDE.md` is "pinning the most important reference material in the most visible spot on the desk ahead of time," and Sub Agent is "distributing the work to different people at different desks."

### II. Instruction Quality > Model Parameters

- Many beginners mistakenly believe that "the stronger the model, the better the results will naturally be." But in practice, **clear, specific instructions with well-defined boundaries** often have a bigger impact on result quality than "which model tier" is used;
- Three characteristics of a good instruction:
  1. **A clear goal**: state clearly "what does 'done' look like," rather than a vague "help me optimize this";
  2. **Clear boundaries**: state clearly "what must not be touched, what must be preserved";
  3. **A provided reference**: giving an example or reference standard is more effective than an abstract description.

**Comparison example (classroom demonstration):**

| Vague Instruction | Clear Instruction |
|---|---|
| "Help me write some marketing copy" | "Write 3 Xiaohongshu-style headlines for this thermos targeted at students, each no more than 20 characters, with an emotional hook, referencing the style in @viral-headline-examples.md" |

### III. Summary of This Chapter's Best-Practices Checklist

Combining everything learned in 2.1–2.5, guide learners to summarize it into an actionable checklist together:

**Workspace Management**
- Each project has its own independent folder, with a standardized structure (handbook / raw materials / outputs kept separate);
- Workspace scope should be "just enough" — avoid starting within an overly broad directory;

**Context Management**
- Write project background into `CLAUDE.md` to avoid re-explaining it every time;
- Put long-term data into a Claude Project, and put temporary materials into a single conversation;
- Make good use of `/clear` and `/compact` when a conversation grows long or when switching tasks;

**Task Division**
- For complex, multi-stage tasks, consider using Sub Agent to split responsibilities;
- Don't over-design simple tasks — avoid "using a sledgehammer to crack a nut";

**Continuous Learning**
- Build a habit of regularly checking the official documentation and changelog;
- Make good use of the method of "using Claude to learn about Claude."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Will I get an error if the context window fills up?**
A: Usually the system will automatically handle it as it approaches the limit (such as suggesting compression or automatically truncating earlier content) — it won't suddenly crash. However, it will affect the quality of responses, so it's recommended to manage context proactively rather than waiting until it's triggered passively.

**Q: Do I need to write out a long CLAUDE.md before I can start every task?**
A: No. CLAUDE.md can start with just a few simple lines and be gradually refined through iteration as you use it — there's no need to strive for a perfect result in one step.
