# 0.4 | Role-Play Instruction Design: Building a Custom Persona for Claude

## Learning Objectives

- Understand why role-play instructions (Persona Prompting) can significantly change output quality and style;
- Master the core elements of designing a role-play instruction;
- Complete a hands-on comparison of at least two different role setups, and directly experience the difference.

## Suggested Duration

25 minutes (hands-on walkthrough)

## Prerequisites

- 0.1 (Structured Prompt Framework) must be completed. Role-play instructions are an intensified way of using the "Context" element from the four elements.

## Course Outline

### I. Why "Role" Can Change Output Quality

- For the same task, having Claude answer as a "senior lawyer" versus as a "junior intern who just started the job" will produce noticeably different levels of rigor, word choice, and focus;
- Reason: a role setup gives Claude a more specific "point of view" — what details to pay attention to, what level of professional depth to use, what tone to adopt — all of these naturally converge to a more appropriate range depending on the role;
- This isn't "playing pretend" — it's a technique that genuinely improves the relevance and precision of the output.

### II. Core Elements of a Role-Play Instruction

```
1. Who         —— Who is this role? What professional background/experience do they have?
2. Perspective —— What is this role's core focus when looking at the problem?
3. Tone        —— How does this role speak, and what word choices does it favor?
4. Scope       —— In this task, what is this role responsible for, and what is it not?
```

**Example:**

```
Who: You are a UX designer with 10 years of experience
Perspective: Always evaluate the design from the angle of "will a first-time user
  be confused"
Tone: Direct, focused on practical actionability, no vague platitudes
Scope: Only assess this page's usability issues; visual aesthetics don't need
  to be evaluated
```

### III. Hands-on: A Role Comparison Experiment

Give learners a fixed task and run it once with two different role setups, so they can directly compare the difference:

**Task:** "Please review this product description copy."

**Role A:**

```
Who: You are a rigorous, compliance-focused legal reviewer
Perspective: Focus on whether the copy contains exaggerated claims or promises
  that can't be verified
```

**Role B:**

```
Who: You are a seasoned copywriter skilled at creating emotional resonance
Perspective: Focus on whether the copy's appeal and emotional impact are
  strong enough
```

Have learners compare the two outputs: for the same piece of copy, the two roles give completely different directions of feedback — discuss "which scenario calls for which role."

### IV. Applicable Scenarios for Role-Play Instructions

- **Multi-perspective review**: have different professional roles each review the same piece of content (legal perspective, user perspective, competitor perspective) — this is more targeted than a single request for a "comprehensive review";
- **Maintaining a consistent expression style**: set a fixed role for an assistant used over the long term (echoing the "persona" concept in Chapter 3's "personal content context"), so the output style stays consistent every time;
- **Breaking out of habitual thinking patterns**: have Claude play the role of a "picky critic" who actively looks for flaws — this surfaces real problems more effectively than simply asking for "some suggestions."

### V. A Reminder About the Boundaries of Role-Play

- Role-play instructions are meant to adjust the **perspective and style of a response**; they should not be used to have Claude play a role that could be used to generate harmful or deceptive content, or to try to bypass its safety guidelines;
- A good role setup serves the task's own professionalism and precision — it isn't there for "costume-play" or to mislead.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How detailed should a role setup be?**
A: The core is the two most critical items, "Who + Perspective" — tone and scope can be filled in as needed. Simple tasks don't need all four elements fully specified every time; for important tasks or ones that need professional depth, it's worth being specific.

**Q: Can I give multiple roles at once and have them "converse" or "debate"?**
A: Yes, you can try this — for example, "Evaluate this proposal's feasibility separately from the perspectives of a product manager and an engineer." This kind of multi-role comparison can help you evaluate a decision more comprehensively, and is an advanced use of role-play instructions.

## Homework

For the same real task, design at least two different role-play instructions and test each one separately. Record the specific differences in focus and style between the two outputs, and think about which role better fits your actual needs.
